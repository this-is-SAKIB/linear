Preliminary Bullet Problems

Fix three non-null vectors in $\mathbb{R}^4$, study a subspace $W$, and build a polynomial subspace isomorphic to $U$.

  1. • Fix three non-null vectors $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^4$ such that only $\mathbf{u}$ belongs to the subspace $U = \langle\, (4,3,2,1),\; (-1,0,0,0) \,\rangle$. Explain why the chosen $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{w}$ do not belong to $U$.
    A vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^4$ belongs to $U$ if and only if it can be written as $\alpha\,(4,3,2,1) + \beta\,(-1,0,0,0)$ for some $\alpha,\beta \in \mathbb{R}$. Equivalently, the last three coordinates of $\mathbf{x}$ must satisfy the ratios imposed by the generator $(4,3,2,1)$: since $(-1,0,0,0)$ only affects the first coordinate, any $\mathbf{x} \in U$ must have $x_2 : x_3 : x_4 = 3\alpha : 2\alpha : \alpha$, i.e.\ $x_4 \neq 0 \Rightarrow x_2 = 3x_4$ and $x_3 = 2x_4$.
  2. •• Exhibit two different bases of the subspace $W \subseteq \mathbb{R}^4$ spanned by $\mathbf{w}$, $\mathbf{u}$, and $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$ (using the vectors chosen in the previous part).
    Form the matrix whose rows are $\mathbf{w},\,\mathbf{u},\,\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}$ and row-reduce. The non-zero rows give one basis; replace any one basis vector by a suitable linear combination to produce a second distinct basis.
  3. ••• Show a subspace $S$ of the polynomial ring $\mathbb{R}[x]$ that is isomorphic to $U$. If possible, exhibit two non-null linear maps from $S$ to $U$ that are not isomorphisms.
    Since $\dim U = 2$, any 2-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}[x]$ works, e.g.\ $S = \langle 1,\, x \rangle$. A linear map $T: S \to U$ fails to be an isomorphism if it is either not injective (kernel non-trivial) or not surjective — both are possible for non-zero maps between spaces of the same finite dimension only when the map is neither injective nor surjective.

1 — Parametric Linear System

For $a \in \mathbb{R}$, define the matrices below and solve the system for all values of $a$ that make it solvable.

  1. Let $$E = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -a \\ -a & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \quad F = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \quad G = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}.$$ Solve the linear system $$EF \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = G$$ for all values of $a$ that make it solvable. (Products are row-by-column.)
    Compute $M = EF$ symbolically, then analyse $\det(M)$ to find the values of $a$ for which the system $M\mathbf{x}=G$ is consistent (check both $\det(M) \neq 0$ and, when $\det(M)=0$, whether $G \in \operatorname{Im}M$).

2 — Vector Orthogonal to a Line in the Plane

Work in the Euclidean plane with Cartesian coordinate system $(O, [\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}])$.

  1. Show the components of a vector $\mathbf{v}$ having length 3 and orthogonal to the line $r\!: \; 2x + y - 3 = 0$. Then determine whether the angle formed by $\mathbf{v}$ and the vector $\mathbf{w} = 20\,\mathbf{i} - 40\,\mathbf{j}$ is acute.
    The normal direction to $ax + by + c = 0$ is $(a, b)$. Scale $(2, 1)$ to unit length, then multiply by 3. The angle with $\mathbf{w}$ is acute if and only if $\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w} > 0$. There are two possible choices of $\mathbf{v}$ (opposite directions); check both.

3 — Lines and Plane in Euclidean Space

Work in Euclidean 3-space with coordinate system $(O, [\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}])$.

  1. Let $r$ be the line through $A \equiv (0,2,1)$ parallel to $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{i} - 3\mathbf{j} + 2\mathbf{k}$.

    (a) Show the parametric equations of a line $s$ that is orthogonal to and intersects $r$.
    (b) If it exists, show the equation of a plane $\pi$ parallel to both $r$ and $s$ such that $\pi \cap r = \emptyset$.
    For (a): choose a point $B$ not on $r$; the foot of the perpendicular from $B$ to $r$ gives the intersection point, and the direction of $s$ is $B - \text{foot}$. For (b): $\pi$ must be parallel to both direction vectors, so its normal is $\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{d}_s$; then choose the offset so that $r$ does not lie on $\pi$.

4 — Eigenspaces and Diagonalisation

Work with the standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$ throughout.

  1. Let $A$ be the matrix representing the linear map $$f : (x_1, x_2, x_3) \;\longmapsto\; (0,\; 2x_3,\; 2x_2),$$ with respect to the standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

    Find a basis of each eigenspace of $A$. If it exists, exhibit a diagonal matrix similar to $A$.
    Write down $A$ from the map definition, compute $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$, find eigenvalues, then solve $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ for each eigenvalue. $A$ is diagonalisable iff the geometric multiplicities sum to 3 (i.e.\ $\mathbb{R}^3$ has a basis of eigenvectors).
